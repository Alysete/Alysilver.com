Case study · AI governance

Building AI governance from scratch at a civic recreation startup

Role: governance consultantScope: data, privacy, first AI featureControl: 7-day data retention

The situation

A civic recreation startup had no data governance function. It collected personal data (names, emails, dates of birth) and was about to launch its first AI feature.

What I did

  • Assessed all personal data collected and reviewed applicable law in every state the company operated in, plus GDPR and CPRA.
  • Wrote and iterated the company's first Privacy Policy and Terms of Use.
  • When the team built an in-house people-counting model to show users how busy a court was, I built a governance checklist to document it. It covered what the backend and frontend did, which policies it touched, and what risks it created for the company, its users, and its municipal partners.
  • The core risks were identity and personal security, since the data touched on presence and location patterns.

Controls, in detail

  • Data minimization: blurring applied to raw footage.
  • Retention limit: 7-day data retention window, then deletion.
  • Access control: municipal partners could review footage within that window. Nothing was retained or shared beyond it.

Transparency, on two tracks

  • Municipal partners: contract language disclosing the AI feature, so there were no surprises.
  • End users: updated Privacy Policy and Terms of Use, plus an in-app notice explaining that an AI feature was in use and why. Not just buried in legal text.
What this means for your organization

Most small teams don't need a heavy compliance program. They need a clear record of what their AI does, the risks it creates, and controls sized to those risks.

Case study · Bias and fairness

Finding and fixing a skin tone gap in avatar data capture

Role: technical program managerSetting: metaverse research companyGate: attribute checklist

The situation

At a metaverse research company, I worked hands-on with engineers building hardware systems that captured human data for avatar research.

The problem

I asked how the systems performed for people with disabilities, different skin tones, tattoos, and piercings. No one had concrete answers. The operators who ran sessions with participants knew some appearances weren't captured correctly. That data was effectively excluded from the foundational models. It was a known issue, but undocumented.

What I did

  • Pulled together a cross-rank group of researchers, operators, and managers. The consistent gap: no one had documented what could and couldn't be captured.
  • Co-founded the Data Diversity Program with a mission statement and values.
  • Built a checklist covering the full range of physical attributes, then used it to audit existing capture systems.

A concrete finding

The capture suite wasn't reliably capturing darker skin tones, so the data wasn't usable for avatars. We traced it to lighting: at certain brightness levels, darker skin reflected light back as a sheen. We ran lighting experiments with the research team and validated the fix with participants who had darker skin tones. The pipeline then produced accurate avatars for them.

The outcome

The checklist became a standing gate. Every capture system had to pass testing across a wider range of appearances before sign-off.

What this means for your organization

Bias often lives in undocumented gaps that the people closest to the work already know about. Asking them, and writing it down, is where fairness work starts.

Case study · Model evaluation

Release-blocking evaluation and benchmarking for a security AI model

Role: AI evaluation and QA leadSetting: early-stage startupGate: false positives over 10% block release

The situation

I led evaluation for a desktop security product at an early-stage startup. It flagged phishing and unsafe links in real time, and flagged personal data being copied into applications.

What I built

  • Two testing scripts, built with Claude Code. One benchmarked the model against a known set of false positives and negatives to catch drift or regression. The other scanned a sample of the top 1M websites, which are presumed safe, to surface new false positives.
  • A human-in-the-loop review: I confirmed each new false positive manually and added it to a known-FP database so engineering could retrain the model.
  • Periodic re-checks of older false positives to confirm whether the model had improved.
The control

Sites known to be safe should never be flagged. If more than 10% of a known-safe benchmark set gets flagged, the release is blocked until the cause is found.

The control worked

On the 1.0 release, a major UX update shipped with a model update and false positives spiked above 10%. We traced the cause. Most flags were subdomains on known-safe hosting platforms like GitHub and Substack, so we allowlisted those hosting patterns. That resolved most flagged cases and brought the rate back under the threshold.

What this means for your organization

A control only matters if it can stop a release. Set a measurable threshold, track it continuously, and re-check whenever the model or product changes.

Case study · Risk tiering

Risk-tiered and collaborative safety review for autonomous vehicle requirements

Role: program manager, Product teamSetting: autonomous vehicle companyTier: pedestrian = highest

The situation

At an autonomous vehicle company, I was the program manager on the Product team delivering technical requirements for autonomous vehicle behavior.

The problem

Requirements moved through a spreadsheet in sequence: Product drafted, Engineering signed off, then Safety and Legal reviewed. That took weeks. Safety or Legal often flagged issues after Engineering had started building, so the cycle restarted. The churn was expensive and demoralizing.

What I did

  • Proposed moving review into Jira, with Product, Engineering, and Safety/Legal all signing off before engineering work began.
  • Product pushed back hard, worried about overhead. I showed that upfront sign-off would be faster overall because it removed the late rework cycle.
  • Built the full Jira project: workflows, required sign-off gates so nothing advanced without every team's approval, and a bulk upload of existing requirements. Then onboarded Safety and Legal, who had never used Jira.
  • Ran a trial on P0 requirements first. Once Product saw less rework, they were fully on board, and it became the standard process.

Risk tiering in practice

Not every requirement carried the same risk. Pedestrian interaction requirements were the highest tier and got more review time and scrutiny by default.

One example covered vehicle behavior around pedestrians with disabilities in crosswalks. Product and Engineering wanted to move forward. Safety and Legal needed more time for edge cases. We worked it through in a biweekly working session.

The resolution

Signed off with additional safety controls, which could be revisited once the technology had proven itself.

What this means for your organization

Controls shouldn't be static. Match their intensity to current risk and demonstrated maturity, and define what would trigger a review.
